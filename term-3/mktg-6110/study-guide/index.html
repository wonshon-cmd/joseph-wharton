Lecture 1

Marketing & the Firm · STP · Positioning

The big idea: “We need a CMO who thinks like a CFO”

Core lesson: Pringles (P&G, 1968) Don't sell what you can make. MAKE WHAT YOU CAN SELL. P&G built a chip that wouldn't break, had a 1-year shelf life, and tied competitors in blind taste tests. It launched with heavy advertising and a 10% price premium. Share hit 25% through trial, but repeat purchase was very low and share fell to 7% by 1978. Consumers inferred an artificial taste from the uniform shape, the can, and the preservatives. P&G had built attributes that mattered to the company and retailers, not to the consumer.

Marketing

Create value
“What do customers need?”

Operations

Deliver value
“Can we deliver it reliably?”

Finance

Capture value
“Does it create lasting shareholder value?”

Extraordinary results happen when all three answer yes at the same time. Most firms operate in silos: Marketing promises what Ops can't deliver, Finance cuts budgets that undermine long-term value, Ops scales without understanding demand.

✔ When it works: Rolls-Royce “Power by the Hour”Rolls-Royce sells guaranteed flying hours, not engines (Marketing). Predictive maintenance makes that promise real (Ops). Rolls-Royce absorbs maintenance risk and gets paid more when the engine needs less servicing (Finance). Three functions, one incentive.
✘ When it doesn't (yet): PelotonIn 2020 demand surged, and leadership misread the surge as permanent. Finance approved big capex and Ops scaled up just as the surge faded. A safety recall then damaged the brand. By 2026, under new leadership, Peloton had its first profitable year, though growth is still fragile. Same functions, different alignment.

What substantiates the POD?

It is an internal statement of the strategy's essence and is externally manifested in the 4Ps. Positioning requires making choices; pick a position that is defensible. Digital twins / synthetic respondents (Toubia 2025)

Premise & when they make senseLLMs calibrated to mimic specific individuals. Use when: ① high-stakes (worth the effort), ② complex or dynamic environments (study the otherwise impossible), ③ enough high-quality data.
The factsOnly ≈ 0.2 correlation between twins and humans. They replicate only about half of experimental effects and overestimate effect sizes; results are prompt-sensitive. Reported 0.8–0.9 correlations come from within-person averages, not across-person prediction.
Why they deviate
  • Hyper-accuracy and hyper-rationality
  • Insufficient individuation: shrunk toward the base
  • Training data skews higher income, higher education, more moderate politics
  • Stereotyping: over-rely on demographics
Good uses ✔ early directional read, idea generation, comparing concepts, pre-testing, costly scenarios.
Bad uses ✘ predicting what one specific person will do, precise demand estimates, high-stakes medical/legal/financial decisions, representative claims without human evidence.
Improve: richer input (adaptive interviews, behavioral data); calibrate on a mixed human + synthetic panel.

Customer Lifetime Value: the Marketing–Finance interface

DefinitionCLV = net present value of all future profit a customer generates over the relationship, less the acquisition cost. Customers are assets; measure their value and use it to decide how much to spend acquiring, retaining, and cross-selling.

Marketing

Sets the ceiling on acquisition cost: CAC ≤ CLV. Anything else destroys value.

Operations

Sets service levels by segment. Not all customers deserve the same resources.

Finance

Turns relationships into a measurable asset, so marketing spend is defensible.

Building blocks

The formula (constant margin & retention, infinite horizon)

Maximizing CLV: two levers

Lever 1: Costs & fit

Decrease fixed or variable cost to serve (self-service, referrals, WOM). Identify low-CLV customers and “fire” them; not every customer is worth keeping.

Lever 2: Revenue & loyalty

Identify high-CLV customers and invest (premium tiers, exclusivity). Grow revenue per customer (cross-sell, upsell, subscriptions, frequency). Invest in retention (lock-in, ecosystems, membership, service).

CLV in action (Zodiac): Acquisition: allocate the channel budget by CLV, run lookalike campaigns, lead-score prospects. Retention: predict churn, win back high-risk customers, reactivate lapsed ones. Loyalty: segment by expected future transactions, use different levers by tier, cross-sell to high “probability active” customers.

Connect to the sample essay: subscription perksFree shipping, gifts, and exclusive deals raise sign-ups and retention (r↑). But they can erode margins (m↓), train customers to expect discounts, attract deal-seekers who only buy on offers (low incremental value, adverse selection), and cannibalize full-price sales. CLV = m × r/(1+i−r) shows the trade-off directly.

Marketing Math: Margin, Break-Even, BECR, Chain, EVC, wCPM

Spirit of the mathThese tools are “not formulae for success but a ladder to success.” They may not give the answer, but they rule out incorrect strategies. There is no one right answer, only better and worse ones: the pieces fit, assumptions are justified, and sensitivity analysis tests robustness. The challenge is the inputs (Garbage In, Garbage Out).

Margin analysis: the value chain

Variable costs change with volume (manufacturing, shipping, commissions). Fixed costs stay the same regardless of volume (executive salaries, rent, insurance, overhead; for marketing: advertising, sales force).

Break-even formulas

TBEV = Total Fixed Cost ÷ Unit Contribution IBEV (fixed-cost action) = Δ Expenditure ÷ Unit Contribution IBEV (margin change) = Old Volume × (Old UC − New UC) ÷ New UC → extra units needed equivalently: total new volume needed = Old Volume × Old UC ÷ New UC IBEV (both) = [Δ Expenditure + Old Volume × ΔUC] ÷ New UC BECR = New product UC ÷ Old product UC (actual cannibalization must be < BECR)
ZYX examplesStreet $9, retailer margin $4 → price $5; VC $3 → UC $2.
• TBEV: $60M fixed ÷ $2 = 30M units
• +$15M ads: ÷ $2 = 7.5M extra
• $0.50 price cut on 90M: 90M × 0.5 ÷ 1.5 = 30M extra (to 120M)
• Both: (15M + 45M) ÷ 1.5 = 40M > 37.5M, because a smaller margin must pay for the ads
• New product at $4, cost $2.50: BECR = 1.5 / 2 = 75%
BECR, intuitivelyThe maximum share of the new product's volume that may come from the old product without losing money (the other 1 − BECR must be new volume from competitors or market growth).

Reciprocal: 2.00 / 1.50 = 1.33. For every lost old unit you must sell 1.33 new units: 1 cannibalized + 0.33 truly incremental.

Large BECR is good. If new UC > old UC, BECR > 100%, so there's no problem.

Branding: what brands do, measuring them, managing a portfolio

Big ideaA brand is the only part of the product that's truly, durably unique. Functional attributes get copied; what customers believe about you doesn't. That's why it's called the “modern patent.” A brand brings the positioning to life.

Formally

A proprietary trademark for a product or service.

Conceptually

A “contract”/promise of specific benefits, quality, value. A relationship.

Realistically

“No longer what we tell consumers it is; it's what consumers tell each other it is.” (Scott Cook)

Market value: what the sim scores

Market value ≈ Σ projected free cash flow, discounted at WACC − debt Game uses the MAX of: ① DCF ② CLV / private-brand-adjusted value ③ liquidation value Value is created only if ROIC > WACC (the spread matters, not margin or growth alone)

Lead on 2, parity on 2 ✔

WACC = blended return investors require. Higher WACC → heavier discounting → lower value; a 1–2 pt WACC swing moves value 20–30%. Debt is cheap at first (tax advantage), but too much raises risk. Pricing: EVC, cost floors, competition, price discrimination

The pricing range

EVC: the ceilingThe highest price a customer should pay, quantified in the customer's terms and narrative. Common in B2B (software, services, energy) where value is measurable. In consumer markets, WTP is more about branding and is harder to quantify. Showing EVC raises the customer's reference point: even if you don't get that price, you end up higher (EV vs. gas savings, energy-efficient appliances).
The pool pill (Prof. Raju's 20,000-gal pool)Drain $20 + refill $200 = $220 plus headache. A student said $450 to include his time. But the realistic price is far lower (~$10–20) because of: ① Fairness: it's ~$0.50 of chlorine, and people won't pay $220 for that. ② Competitive rivalry: a $200 margin invites a rival at $100, then $50… With no competition and no fairness concern (think pharma, justified by R&D), you could charge $220 or more.

The cost floor: two ways to think about cost

① Markup pricing

Cost of goods + a markup %. Costco ~11%, Walmart ~25%. (Pivot or Perish's “price markup” is over cost.)

② Break-even-based

Each unit also carries its share of fixed costs + variable cost + margin. This truly covers your costs. You can do both in BeWood.
The widget problem: “cover the fixed” (there IS a right answer) Price $7, 1,000 units; variable cost $1/unit ($1,000), fixed cost $3,000, overhead $1,500 → total $5,500 → profit $1,500. Sales fall to 750: revenue $5,250 − $750 − $4,500 = $0. Raise or lower price? → LOWER. The cost structure is fixed-cost-heavy, so you need volume to cover the fixed costs. Scenario analysis: raising the price loses money whether volume falls 5% or 33%; lowering it makes money in both good and bad scenarios.
Fixed-cost-heavy → LOWER price, drive volume ("cover the fixed") Variable-cost-heavy → RAISE price, you earn the margin on every unit e.g., Jesse's sushi omakase: high ingredient & labor (variable) cost → price keeps going up; the cheaper front-of-restaurant brings volume to cover the building (fixed)

Company factors & competition

Company factors
  1. Maximize profit: charge each person their highest WTP (price discrimination, legally and ethically)
  2. Cover costs: depends on cost structure
  3. Product-line consistency: premium can't be priced below the cheap version
Competition = game theory: can they match your price (their costs/break-even) and will they? Warby Parker entered at $95 vs Luxottica's ~$300 (Luxottica owns brands and retailers, vertically integrated). Luxottica could have crushed Warby but wouldn't: matching would put too much margin at risk and it couldn't raise prices back, so it ignored the “little Wharton MBA student.” Neil Blumenthal credits Prof. Raju with the pricing.

④ EXTENSIONS & LUXURY ★ sample essay
  • Why extend: ① cheaper (leverage equity) ② move the brand (Mountain Man → young drinkers)
  • Two-way test: brand adds value to the extension AND the extension adds value back. Never extend a weak brand or weaken the original → otherwise use a new brand.
  • Horizontal (new category, same price: Clorox wipes, Lulu men) vs vertical (BMW; going down hurts the top; Toyota→Lexus; Kia)
  • Luxury: does it reinforce or dilute prestige/scarcity? Too accessible → loses cachet → loses the premium. Pierre Cardin ✘ vs Hermès/Gucci ✔. Long-term equity > short-term revenue.
  • Stay fresh: Schweppes heritage · Pepsi music · Porsche gaming · LV × Supreme · silver bullet

▸ e.g. Clorox → wipes · Starbucks → bottled Frappuccino, ice cream · Lululemon women → men · BMW 760Li $160k vs 128i $31.5k · Mountain Man light beer · Pierre Cardin ✘ · Hermès, Gucci ✔ · Schweppes · Pepsi · Porsche · LV × Supreme · Amazon luxury (doubtful)

⑤ SUBSCRIPTION PERKS ★ sample essay
  • Unintended: ① margin erosion ② trained to expect discounts ③ deal-seekers → low incremental value ④ cannibalize full-price sales
  • CLV = m·r/(1.1−r): $100 @ 80% = $267; perks → $75 @ 85% = $255 (worse); need ~86% retention to break even
  • Kahn: loyal = less price-sensitive → “I raise price” (American Airlines). Subscriptions = 3-part tariff.
  • Fix: tiers, non-price perks, measure incrementality. Costco ~92% renewal → 5.1×

▸ e.g. Amazon Prime · Costco (92.2% US/Can renewal, $1.33B fees/qtr) · American Airlines miles (“3 peanuts”) · Netflix · newspapers · Dropbox/AI freemium · Dollar Diaper Club

⑥ MASS vs TWO SEGMENTS ★ sample essay (25% each)

⑦ MEASURING BRANDS 🎤
  • Customer-based: awareness (recognition, recall, top-of-mind, latency) → associations (conjoint) → emotion (neuromarketing) → loyalty (purchases). Price premium = branded − unbranded price.
  • BAV: Differentiation (margins), Relevance (trial), Esteem (loyalty), Knowledge. Strength = D+R; Stature = E+K.
  • Opposite models: awareness first (Coke, Bud) vs passion first (BAV, Lululemon). Known but undifferentiated = weak (TV Guide); niche may not scale.
  • Interbrand: brand earnings, discounted; strong brand → low rate → high value. Used to justify spend to the CFO, in M&A, in lawsuits. Coke $68B → Apple #1.

▸ e.g. Coke $68B → Apple $470.9B #1 · Nvidia +116% · Tesla −35% · perfume 95% vs utilities 0% · Lululemon (BAV) · TV Guide
