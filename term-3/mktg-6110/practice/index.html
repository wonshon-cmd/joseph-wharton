Topic ① · Sample MC #1

Kahn Retailing Success Matrix

📄 SAMPLE How it was asked “According to the Kahn Success Matrix, Amazon's strategy could be characterized as:”
✔ Best at Frictionless, leveraging that advantage to be the best at Low Price, and fair value at Brand and Customer Experience
✘ Best at Customer Experience and brand, fair value on low price and frictionless
✘ Best at Low Price and Brand, fair value at frictionless and customer experience
✘ Best at Customer experience, leveraging that to be best at Brand, fair value at low price and frictionless

Learn it

The big picture in plain EnglishProf. Kahn wrote The Shopping Revolution because many stores were failing (the “retail apocalypse”). Her question: why do some retailers win? Her answer: customers go where they get the best value on what they care about. There are only four basic ways to offer superior value. Winners are the best at one, use it to become best at a second, and are “good enough” at the other two.
QuadrantPlain meaning
BrandThe products themselves pull you in: strong brands, great merchandise, design. Nordstrom, Lululemon, Warby Parker, luxury.
ExperientialThe shopping itself is enjoyable: fun, discovery, service, community. Sephora, Apple Store, Costco's treasure hunt.
Low PriceCheapest, through super-efficient operations. Walmart (EDLP), Costco, T.J.Maxx.
FrictionlessEasiest: no lines, fast delivery, everything in one place, personalized with data. Amazon.

The rules (memorize these four lines)

  1. Survive: be at least at fair value (customers' minimum expectation) in all four. Fall below in any one and customers leave (RadioShack went out of business).
  2. Lead: be the best in one quadrant, then leverage that strength to become best in a second (the “arrow” strategy).
  3. Don't overspend on the other two. “Good enough” is enough; extra money there takes resources from your strengths.
  4. The bar keeps rising: when a leader offers something better (2-day delivery), it becomes everyone's new minimum.

Who wins where (the two-quadrant strategies) 📊 SLIDES/BOOK

RetailerStarted best at…Leveraged into…How
AmazonFrictionlessLow Price“Everything store,” data, fast delivery → scale lets it guarantee very low prices
WalmartLow PriceFrictionlessEDLP + supply-chain efficiency → bought Jet.com for omnichannel
Warby Parker / ZaraBrandLow PriceHip brand + cut out the middleman
Costco / T.J.MaxxLow PriceExperiential“Treasure hunt”: you never know what you'll find
Luxury brandsBrandExperientialTop products need luxurious experiences too
SephoraExperientialFrictionlessTry-it-yourself stores + huge loyalty program across store and online
Common trapAmazon and Walmart win in the same two quadrants (Frictionless + Low Price) but start from opposite strengths. Read “best at X, leveraging that to be best at Y” carefully: the first named is the starting strength.

In the simulation (Pivot or Perish) 🎤 CLASS

You run Getchells = Macy's, a mid-tier department store “stuck in the middle.” Kahn in class: “the mid-tier are stuck in the middle… it's the ones stuck in the middle” that fail; multi-brand stores that pick a clear strategy (Sephora, T.J.Maxx, Walmart, Costco) win. Fair value = 75% of the leader. Your spending moves you only if you outspend competitors over time (“sluggish”). Levers: online → Frictionless; shopping experience → Experiential; vendor management → Low Price; brand quality → Brand; customer service → both Frictionless and Experiential; analytics = booster. Past teams did best leading in 2 with parity in the other 2.
Topic ② · Sample MC #2

What makes a good segmentation?

📄 SAMPLE How it was asked “A marketing manager divided her market by residential geography into an eastern and a western segment. Was this a good segmentation scheme?”
✔ It depends on whether the consumers within each division were relatively homogeneous in their reactions to marketing variables and the two divisions were sufficiently different from each other
✘ Yes, east and west are distinct, no overlap · ✘ No, geography is never good · ✘ No, hard to observe/reach · ✘ It depends on whether there is sufficient heterogeneity within each division

Learn it

Plain EnglishSegmentation = splitting a market into groups of customers so you can serve each group well. Why bother? Because different people want different things. If you design for the “average” customer, you design for nobody. Kahn's slide: “Serving the average buyer well serves no one.”

The test for a GOOD segmentation (this is what the sample tests)

✔ Homogeneous WITHIN

People in the same group react the same way to the 4Ps: same price sensitivity, same message appeal, same channels.

✔ Heterogeneous BETWEEN

The groups are meaningfully different from each other, so they deserve different marketing.

Also: identifiable (can you tell who's in it?), reachable (can you get to them?), big and profitable enough, and actionable (“segmentation is only valuable if it is actionable”).

Why each wrong option is wrong • “East and west are distinct, no overlap”: being separate on a map doesn't mean they behave differently.
• “Geography is never good”: too absolute. Geography works when needs truly differ by place (climate, culture).
• “Hard to observe and reach”: geography is actually very easy to see and reach.
• “Sufficient heterogeneity WITHIN”: backwards. You want sameness within, difference between. Watch for this flip!

What variables can you segment on? 📊 SLIDES

TypeConsumer examplesB2B examples
Geographiczip, region, countryzip, region, country
Demographicage, gender, income, family size, educationindustry, company size, sales, R&D budget
Psychographicpersonality, lifestyle, media habitscorporate culture, power structure
Attitudinalbenefits sought, price sensitivity, brand loyaltybenefits sought, price sensitivity, supplier loyalty
Behavioralusage occasion, usage rate, repeat buying / CLVapplications, order size, existing relationships
Benefit segmentation = best, but trickyPeople buy different products because they want different benefits. But you can't see what someone wants. So find visible variables that go along with the benefit sought (which aisle they shop, how often they buy).
Why gender is a weak shortcut“Tactical, but is it strategic?” ① Stereotypes everyone in the group. ② Shrinks your market and may alienate people. ③ People fit neat categories less and less. ④ Can seem unfair. Note: gender IS easy to observe, so that isn't the problem.

Topic ③ · Sample MC #3

Brand Architecture: House of Brands vs Branded House

📄 SAMPLE How it was asked “…a firm very concerned about managing risk across their portfolio of brands. They have a division that invests in new, risky technologies; they want to make sure their more established brands are protected from problems that may arise if these new technologies fail. Which brand strategy would you expect?”
✔ House of brands · ✘ Branded house · ✘ Brand repositioning · ✘ Brand identity

Learn it

Plain EnglishBrand architecture = how a company with many products organizes their names. Does everything carry ONE company name, or does each product get its OWN name? It's a trade-off between saving money by sharing one famous name and protecting each brand from the others' problems.
✔ Pros✘ Cons
House of BrandsEach brand can own its own segment; a failure doesn't spread; no dilution of other namesMost expensive (build every brand from scratch); brands may compete with each other
EndorsedBorrows the parent's credibility; keeps its own identity; can “graduate” laterBad news about the parent rubs off (negative spillover)
Sub-brandBalances independence with the parent's equityCan dilute the master brand if managed badly
Branded HouseStrongest single brand; cheapest to market (every ad builds one name)One failure can hit the whole brand

What Prof. Kahn said in class 🎤 CLASS

• “A house of brands is all new brands. Like P&G… they own a million different soap brands. But they don't leverage their brand name.” Pharma and luxury do it too.
• Why? “They don't want to dilute the value of any brand name, and they're targeting different segments.” Pharma: “there's too much risk. If one brand, God forbid, kills people, you don't want that to affect the whole portfolio.” ← this is the sample-question logic
• “A branded house is obviously cheaper because I'm just building one brand.”
• Endorsed: Ann Taylor's customers aged, so it launched “Loft by Ann Taylor”; as Ann Taylor got “older and tireder,” they dropped it, and Loft is just Loft.
• Sub-brand: “you're planning on keeping the name the whole time,” but growing into different segments.
• LVMH owns many luxury names without extending them, but gets synergies: back office, advertising dollars, real estate (best store locations, all together), and hiring designers who can move between brands.
• Toyota → Lexus: a new name because stretching “Toyota” into luxury wouldn't work. Kia is trying to move up under its own name.
Goals of ANY brand architectureStrong brandsSynergyClarityLeverage Amazon uses all four types at once (branded house: Amazon Basics; sub-brands: Prime, AWS; endorsed: Audible; house of brands: Zappos, Whole Foods, Twitch).
Why the sample's distractors are wrong“Brand repositioning” (changing what a brand means) and “brand identity” (what it stands for) are not ways to organize a portfolio. When the question says “protect established brands from a risky division,” think separate names = House of Brands.
Topic ④ · Sample short answer #1

Brand Extensions: Long-Term Equity vs Short-Term Revenue

📄 SAMPLE The question & the grading notes “Luxury brands like Gucci or Hermès have been careful with extensions, while others like Pierre Cardin overextended and lost equity. How should managers evaluate the long-term brand equity impact vs. short-term revenue gains of extensions?”

Grading notes: Assess whether the extension reinforces or dilutes prestige/scarcity. Short-term revenue may boost profits, but if it erodes luxury positioning by going too far into the mass market, it's not worth it long term. A good answer recognizes the costs to long-term brand equity, not just maximizing short-term profit, and specifically mentions the luxury issue: a brand that becomes too accessible or too available loses its cachet, and with it its luxury premium.

Learn it

Plain EnglishA brand extension puts a famous name on a new product (Clorox bleach → Clorox wipes). It's tempting because the name already has fans, so the launch is cheap and sales come fast. The danger: if the new product doesn't fit, it waters down what the name means. For luxury, the name means “rare, special, expensive.” Put it on too many cheap things and it stops meaning that, so people stop paying the luxury price.

Why extend? Two advantages 🎤 CLASS

① Leverage the equity

“It's much cheaper to introduce a new product if you come in with the same brand name.”

② Move the brand

Use the new product to refresh or shift what the brand means, e.g., reach younger customers. “This is exactly what Mountain Man is about”: an older-skewing beer trying to win young drinkers.

The two-way test (the heart of every extension answer) 🎤 CLASS

Kahn: “You never extend a brand if it's weak. It's gotta add value… And you also never extend a brand if it's gonna weaken the original one. So you've gotta have two strong links.” If either link fails, launch a new brand: more expensive, but safe.

Horizontal vs vertical extensions 🎤 CLASS 📊 SLIDES

Horizontal

New category, similar price. Clorox → wipes; Starbucks → bottled Frappuccino. Lululemon women → men worked (women bought the men's clothes first; Kahn admits she wrongly predicted Wall Street guys wouldn't wear it). Risk: stretch in too many directions and “you've lost what it originally meant.”

Vertical

Same category, higher or lower price. BMW $160k vs $31.5k. Going down sells volume but can damage the top; going up may not be believable. Toyota made Lexus instead of “luxury Toyota.” Kia is trying to move up under its own name. Can Amazon sell luxury? Kahn has doubts.

Why luxury is special

Scarcity & exclusivity
Hard to get = desirable
Heritage
“You cannot be European luxury unless you're European luxury”: 200 years of legacy is a lasting advantage 🎤
Price premium
Perfume: 90–95% of the purchase decision is the brand 📊
Pierre Cardin vs Hermès/GucciPierre Cardin licensed its name onto many mass products: big short-term licensing revenue, but the name became everywhere and ordinary, so it lost its luxury cachet and premium. Hermès and Gucci extend only into categories and price tiers that keep the brand rare and prestigious.

How to evaluate it: a framework for your essay

  1. Fit with positioning: does the extension reinforce or dilute what the brand stands for (prestige, scarcity)?
  2. Two-way test: does the brand help the product AND does the product help the brand?
  3. Direction: horizontal (new category) or vertical (down-market is the luxury danger zone)?
  4. Short-term gain: extra revenue and a cheaper launch, but beware cannibalization of the core.
  5. Long-term cost: lost price premium and brand value. Measure it: price premium, BAV differentiation/esteem, Interbrand value.
  6. Alternatives: a new brand, an endorsed brand, or a sub-brand if the name can't stretch.

Keep the brand fresh without extending too far 🎤 CLASS

Schweppes was old and tired during the COVID craft-cocktail boom → repositioned on heritage, made modern. Pepsi stays young through music (incubating young musicians). Porsche uses gaming showrooms so young people dream of owning one. Louis Vuitton × Supreme: an edgy collaboration. Last resort: a silver bullet (radical, risky). Problem: “young people are always rebelling about what their parents do.”
Topic ⑤ · Sample short answer #2

Subscription Programs & Perks: Unintended Consequences

📄 SAMPLE The question & the grading notes “Many companies offer free shipping, gifts and exclusive deals as part of their subscription program. What are two potential unintended consequences of including such features?”

Grading notes: Perks can increase signups, but they also risk eroding margins and training customers to expect discounts. Some customers may only make purchases when offers are available, reducing the program's incremental value and possibly cannibalizing full-price sales.

Learn it

Plain EnglishA subscription or membership program (Amazon Prime, Costco membership) charges a fee and gives perks. Companies love them because members stay longer and buy more. But every perk costs money, and perks change how customers behave. Sometimes the program just gives discounts to people who would have bought anyway.

Why companies want subscriptions: the CLV logic 📊 SLIDES

CLV = m × r ÷ (1 + i − r) − AC m = yearly profit per customer r = % who stay each year i = discount rate (10%) AC = cost to acquire

Higher retention (r) makes each customer worth much more: at r = 80% the multiplier is 2.67×; at Costco's ~92% renewal rate it's 5.1×. That's why memberships are so valuable.

The catch, in numbers (worked example)

ScenarioMargin mRetention rCLV = m·r/(1.1−r)
No perks$10080%$266.67
Perks cost $25/yr, retention rises to 85%$7585%$255.00 ↓ worse!
Perks cost $25/yr, retention rises to 88%$7588%$300.00 ↑ better
LessonPerks pay off only if they raise retention enough to beat the margin they cost. Here, retention must rise from 80% to about 86% just to break even. Higher sign-ups alone don't prove the program works.

The unintended consequences (name at least two, explained)

① Margin erosion

Free shipping, gifts, and deals cost money on every order. Heavy users cost the most (lots of free shipping). m goes down.

② Trained to expect discounts

Customers' reference price drops: full price starts to feel unfair, and they wait for deals.

③ Deal-seekers, low incrementality

Some join only for the perks and buy only when offers exist. Much of the “extra” sales would have happened anyway.

④ Cannibalizing full-price sales

Loyal customers who'd pay full price now get the discount: money given away for nothing.

What Prof. Kahn said about loyalty and price 🎤 CLASS

A student suggested discounting loyal high-volume customers. Kahn: “Oh, you do that? Not me. I raise price.” Loyal customers are less price-sensitive. American Airlines in Philly: loyal flyers keep paying “crazy” prices for small benefits (“three peanuts”). “It's really surprising how much price desensitivity happens if you think you're getting a benefit in a certain kind of loyalty program.”
Subscriptions are a three-part tariff (second-degree price discrimination): an intro price to get in, then different prices depending on how much you buy. Freemium is penetration pricing.
Better designs (to end your answer strongly)Tie perks to tiers or spending thresholds · use non-price perks (access, experiences, service) instead of discounts · measure incrementality (compare members vs a control group) · keep full-price integrity, especially for premium or luxury brands.
Topic ⑥ · Sample short answer #3

Mass Market vs Two Distinct Segments

📄 SAMPLE The question & the grading notes “You are CMO of a consumer packaged goods company with limited budget. Choose between: targeting a large, broadly defined mass market with a single positioning, OR targeting two smaller, highly distinct segments with different positions. Compare both options strategically, and discuss how your decision would depend on the costs of differentiation, competitive intensity, and brand equity.”

Grading notes: Mass = lower cost, more efficient media, but less tailored. Multi-segment = more relevant, but more complex and costly. It depends on: (a) competitive intensity: if high and differentiation is critical, go segmented; (b) if differentiation is very costly, assess profitability and make sure the ROI merits it; (c) a very strong brand may sustain a universal mass approach, like Coke. Grading: 25% options compared, 25% cost, 25% competition, 25% brand.

Learn it

Plain EnglishOne message for everyone (mass) is cheap and simple, but it doesn't speak deeply to anyone. Separate messages for separate groups (segmented) speak to each group's real needs, but you pay for two of everything. The right answer depends on how much tailoring costs, how fierce competitors are, and how strong your brand already is.
Option A: Mass, one positioningOption B: Two distinct segments
✔Lower cost; efficient media; economies of scale; simple to runMore relevant; can charge more; harder for rivals to attack; fits “serve real needs”
✘Less tailored, so “serving the average buyer serves no one”; easy for focused rivals to pick off; risk of being stuck in the middleTwo products, ads, and channels; complexity; splits a limited budget; risk of doing both poorly

The three “it depends” factors (each is 25% of the grade)

💰 Cost of differentiation

If tailoring is very expensive, check that each segment's profit (chain model, break-even) justifies it. With a limited budget, the ROI must be clear.

⚔ Competitive intensity

If rivals already target specific segments, a one-size message gets picked apart → segment. If competition is weak, mass may be enough.

🏷 Brand equity

A very strong brand (Coke) can hold a universal appeal. A weak or new brand needs a sharp segment to stand out.

Related ideas Prof. Kahn emphasized 🎤 CLASS 📊 SLIDES

• Two ways to build a brand (class): awareness-first for everyone, the old CPG way (Coke, Budweiser), which still works in mass categories because of the double-jeopardy effect (big brands get more buyers AND more frequent buyers). Versus passion-first in a niche (BAV; Lululemon). The niche approach is “cost efficient and more realistic today” with fragmented media, but may not scale.
• Risk of mass with no differentiation: “you can have very strong brand awareness, but if you don't have that energized differentiation, you actually don't have a strong brand” (stuck in the middle).
• Principle of Selectivity & Concentration: choose and focus. Targeting grid: pick segments that are attractive AND fit your strengths.
• Quidel shows two segments done well; Pringles shows how a sharper target (kids/teens) beat a vague one.
A strong recommendation sentence“With a limited budget, I'd go mass only if our brand is strong and competition is moderate. Otherwise I'd concentrate on the more attractive, better-fitting segment first, prove the ROI, then add the second.”

How to Measure a Brand: 3 Ways and 2 Opposite Models

Plain EnglishIf a brand is valuable, how do you prove it? Kahn showed three ways: look inside customers' heads, use a consulting firm's scorecard (BAV), or put a dollar value on it like a financial asset (Interbrand). Each rests on a different belief about what makes a brand strong.

① Customer-based: what's in the customer's head 🎤 CLASS

StageQuestionHow it's measured (from class)
AwarenessHave you heard of me?Recognition (spot it on a shelf), recall (name brands in a category, aided or unaided), top-of-mind (the first brand you think of), latency (how fast it comes to mind)
AssociationsWhat do you know about me?Asking directly “doesn't work that well,” so use conjoint (infer from choices)
Attitudes / emotionHow do you feel?Neuromarketing: facial recognition, arousal, eye tracking
Attachment / loyaltyAre you loyal?Purchase history, conversion
Conjoint, simplyPeople are “really bad” at saying how important brand or price is. So show them product bundles where brand, price, and features vary, watch which they choose, and “back out” the weights. The brand's weight = its value.
Price premium, simply“How much would you pay for this T-shirt?” vs the same shirt with a brand logo. The difference = the brand's value. Experienced clothing people “can do it in their head.”

② BAV (Young & Rubicam BrandAsset Valuator) 🎤 CLASS 📊 SLIDES

Brand Strength = Differentiation + Relevance (future potential). Brand Stature = Esteem + Knowledge (current standing). These are tracked over time and tied to financial results.

⚡ The key insight from class: the two models are OPPOSITES

Awareness first (customer-based)Passion first (BAV)

③ Interbrand: the brand as a financial asset 🎤 CLASS

Brand value = profits from intangibles × % due to the brand → forecast 5–10 yrs → discount at a rate set by BRAND STRENGTH → + terminal value Strong, stable brand (Coke) → low discount rate → HIGH value · Trendy brand → high discount rate → low value
Two “secret sauce” numbers: ① what % of intangible earnings is due to the brand (perfume ~95%, utilities ~0%; Kahn notes pharma used to be ~10% but changed with COVID vaccine brand choice) ② the discount rate, from a 10-factor brand-strength score (risk-free rate ~5%). “Tiny changes in your discount factor compound.” The same discounting logic is used in CLV and in the simulation's market value.
Why use it? Brand equity isn't on the balance sheet, so it helps the CMO justify marketing to the CFO. Also used for acquisitions, lawsuits (Kahn was an expert witness showing brand value fell after an event), and monitoring. Only for public, profitable firms (non-profits need another metric). Most useful within your industry; the Top 100 list is “a PR mechanism.” Coke $68B (#1 until 2013) → Apple; Tesla −35%; Nvidia the biggest gain ever.
Topic ⑧ · Recorded pricing lecture

Pricing Basics: Ceiling, Floor, and What Decides In Between

Plain EnglishEvery price lands between two walls. The floor is your cost: below it you lose money. The ceiling is the most the product is worth to the customer. Where you land depends on competitors and your own goals.

The ceiling: EVC (Economic Value to the Customer) 🎤 CLASS

EVC = the value of your product to the customer, in dollars, in the customer's terms. It's common in B2B (software, services, energy) where savings are measurable; consumer markets rely more on branding. Even if you don't get the full EVC, showing it raises the customer's reference point (electric cars vs. gas savings, energy-efficient appliances).

The pool pill (Prof. Raju's pool): the class example A 20,000-gallon pool. Draining costs $1 per 1,000 gal = $20; refilling costs $1 per 100 gal = $200. Total $220 plus headache. A pill keeps the water clean under the cover he uses anyway. A student said “$450” (adding his time).
Kahn's point: EVC says $220+, but the real price will be about $10–20, because:
① Fairness: it's about $0.50 of chlorine, and people won't pay $220 for that.
② Competitive rivalry: “a $200 margin on 50 cents of chlorine? I'm gonna come in for $100,” then $50…
With no competition and a strong R&D story (pharma), you could charge near EVC.

The floor: two ways to count cost 🎤 CLASS

Markup

Cost + a %. Costco ~11%, Walmart ~25%.

Break-even based

Each unit also carries a share of fixed costs: “you're really covering your cost if you do it this way.” In BeWood you do both.

⭐ The widget problem: “there is a right answer”

Sell 1,000 at $7Sell 750 at $7
Revenue$7,000$5,250
Variable cost ($1/unit)−$1,000−$750
Fixed + overhead−$4,500−$4,500
Profit$1,500$0
Answer: LOWER the priceCosts are mostly fixed, so you need volume to cover them: “cover the fixed.” Scenario analysis: raising the price loses money whether volume falls 5% or 33%; lowering it makes money in both cases.
The flip side: variable-cost-heavy → RAISEJesse's sushi omakase: expensive fish and chef time on every seat, so he should keep raising the price (“it's always gonna go up”). The cheaper front of the restaurant brings volume to cover the building.

What decides where you land

Company factors
  1. Maximize profit: charge each person their max (legal price discrimination, see topic ⑨)
  2. Cover costs: depends on cost structure
  3. Product-line logic: “I can't have my premium product less than my cheap product”
Competition = game theory: can they match (their costs)? Will they? Warby Parker came in at $95 vs Luxottica's ~$300. Luxottica (owns brands and retailers) could have matched and knocked Warby out, but wouldn't: too much margin at stake, and it couldn't raise prices back. Its CEO just waved off students' questions. (Raju helped Warby's founder set the price.)
Topic ⑨ · Recorded pricing lecture

Price Discrimination: Charging Different People Different Prices (Legally)

Plain EnglishCustomers differ in how much they'll pay. A company makes the most money if each person pays close to their own maximum. Kahn stressed doing this legally and ethically, and keeping people happy, because customers hate prices that feel unfair.
DegreeHow it worksExamples from class
1stA unique price for each person, usually by negotiating up to their maxUsed cars, antiques, art, rugs; B2B deals. ⚠ Control your sales force: they'll cut price rather than lose a sale (BeWood).
2ndCustomers pick a trade-off, usually quantity: buy more, pay less per unitBulk discounts. Two-part tariff: lock in, then pay per use (razor + blades, HP printer + ink, Keurig + pods). Three-part tariff = subscriptions (intro price, then tiers by usage; newspapers). Loyalty programs.
3rdCustomers fall into segments; the less price-sensitive segment pays moreThe menu below ↓

Skimming vs Penetration

Skim = start HIGH for eager buyers, drop over time: Apple launches, fashion (runway → ready-to-wear → T.J.Maxx).
Penetration = start LOW (even free) to hook people, then raise: intro phone plans, coupons for first-timers, freemium (Dropbox, AI tools: “you can't do anything without paying more”).

EDLP vs High-Low

EDLP (Walmart, Best Buy): good prices every day, often with price matching. Most people then stop searching; the few who search get matched. “When they say there's a price match, there probably is a cheaper price out there.”
High-low: loss leaders lure you in; everything else is priced above EDLP. Cherry-pickers buy only the deals, but “most people don't do that.”

Random vs Periodic discounts

Random: you don't know when; coupons, the promo-code box (“I hate that”). Bargain hunters search; others pay full.
Periodic: predictable, but you give something up (early-bird specials, end of season, 3pm movies).

Secondary markets & versioning

Students/seniors get lower prices, but must show ID (a transaction cost so not everyone qualifies).
Versioning (good/better/best): the top tier is priced above its extra benefits for insensitive buyers (first class); the bottom is stripped down; most buy the middle.
Loyalty ≠ discount 🎤 CLASSLoyal customers are less price-sensitive, so “I raise price.” Airline loyalty programs make people pay more for small benefits. That links directly to the sample essay on subscription perks (topic ⑤).
Topic ⑩ · Recorded go-to-market lecture

Place & Channels: How the Product Reaches the Customer

Plain EnglishPlace = where and how people buy: stores, websites, distributors, salespeople. Kahn: it used to be her “least favorite P” (it felt like logistics), but now it's her favorite, because in mature categories with similar products, how you go to market is how you stand out. And 30–50% of the final price sits in the channel.

What channels (stores, distributors) actually do 🎤 CLASS

Sell & educate

Direct vs Indirect

Direct (you sell to the customer yourself)Indirect (through stores or distributors)
✔Full margin · full control of price and experience · you own the customer DATAThey handle distribution · scale · customers can compare brands side by side
✘Hard to scale · you do all the logistics (on your balance sheet) · need strong awareness · may not match how customers decideDouble marginalization · channel conflict and power · less control of price and experience
Double marginalizationEach middleman adds its own markup, so the customer pays more and each party earns less total profit. “Many times the channel makes more money than you do” (as in BeWood).
Channel power & data“Who owns the data… is a critical negotiation point” (Amazon often knows more about your customers than you; Dexcom with Apple Watch). US car dealers are protected by law, so Tesla got around them by arguing “we're not a car,” we're tech. Amazon: “can't live with them, can't live without them.”

⭐ Nike's DTC story (told at length in class)

  1. 2020: Strong brand; everyone's home in athletic wear. Nike pushes direct-to-consumer, cuts margins to retailers, wants to own the data; fully bought into CLV (even bought Prof. Fader's company).
  2. Then: acquiring new customers got hard, plus supply-chain problems. Nike kept only Dick's, Foot Locker, Nordstrom; even Foot Locker couldn't get enough shoes. It pulled out of running stores.
  3. What customers did: didn't go to Nike.com. They bought HOKA, On, New Balance right there. Nike also stopped innovating (over-relied on Air Max and Air Force 1).
  4. 2024: the consultant-CEO is replaced by a product veteran: rebuild wholesale, “back to our DNA.” Lesson: channels really matter.

The retail landscape

• Mid-tier department stores are stuck in the middle (Kohl's, JCPenney, Macy's). Multi-brand still works when done right: Sephora sells the same beauty products as Macy's but differently; T.J.Maxx, Walmart, Costco win.
• DTC brands of 2017–18 (Bonobos, Rent the Runway) couldn't scale, so they moved into Nordstrom and onto Amazon. Luxury did the opposite: pulled out of department stores toward direct or store-within-store to control price and experience. “Which is better? Obviously, like anything in marketing, it depends.” Many do both.
• Mono-brand (sells one brand: Apple, Lululemon) vs multi-brand (department stores). National brands (Coke, Dior) vs private brands (Costco's Kirkland). In the sim, 100% private brand ≈ going mono-brand.
• What killed retailers: “they opened too many stores, they kept too much inventory, they took out too much debt,” and the simulation models exactly this.
Topic ⑪ · Exam-review lecture (pricing part 2) 🎤 CLASS

Price Sensitivity, Bundles, Dynamic Pricing & Subscriptions

Plain EnglishHow much do people react when the price changes? Companies want customers who keep buying even when the price goes up (inelastic). This lecture lists what makes people care less (or more) about price, then covers bundles, prices that change with demand (Uber surge), and subscription and loyalty programs. Exam reminder from this lecture: lecture content only (no cases, no simulation), no math calculations, MC = pick the best answer.
Elastic vs inelasticElastic = a small price change causes a big change in quantity bought (customers are price-sensitive). Inelastic = buyers keep buying when price rises. Marketers want inelastic demand. Every driver below is a lever to push demand toward inelastic.
Scarcity: legal ≠ acceptableScarcity and exclusivity are fine for luxury (Hermès Birkin waitlists, Ferrari invitation-only models). The same tactic at Walmart would be legal but raise ethical and PR concerns. Fairness keeps coming back in pricing.

Bundle pricing

The bundle costs less than the sum of its parts. Customers are price-sensitive on the main item (the computer) and insensitive on peripherals (printer, cables, software, add-ons). Price the headline item sharply and earn margin on the rest. Bundles also make item-by-item comparison harder.

Loss leaders & end-of-aisle

Grocery stores price a few items at or below cost to drive traffic; the rest of the basket carries normal margin (high-low pricing). End-of-aisle displays pull an item away from the shelf of competitors, which removes visible substitutes, so shoppers don't compare.

Dynamic pricing: when is it accepted?

CompanyWhat happenedWhy
Uber (accepted)Surge pricing became normalTransparent, labeled from the start; two-sided market: a higher price brings more drivers, a visible benefit to riders
Wendy's, Walmart (backlash)Talk of demand-based prices drew public angerNo benefit to the customer; looks like exploiting peak demand (gouging)
Airlines, AmazonChange prices constantlyCustomers expect it in those categories
The logicDynamic pricing moves you toward first-degree price discrimination (each buyer pays near their own WTP) and captures consumer surplus. Acceptance depends on fairness. Surge vs. gouging depends on the customer's perceived alternatives and on whether the reason feels fair.

Subscription models (three types)

① Replenishment: refills that arrive automatically (Harry's razors and blades).
② Curated box: Birchbox beauty samples. Most revenue came from customers buying the full-size products they discovered, not the box fee.
③ Tiered loyalty: status levels (airline elite, Sephora-style tiers).
Prof. Raghu Iyengar's loyalty-program study (a beauty company in Asia)
• Endogeneity / self-selection: members spend more, but heavy spenders are the ones who join (like Prime members). Don't read it as cause and effect.
• Measured properly: purchase frequency ↑, basket size slightly ↓, the effect faded over time.
• ~25% of members generated no profit.
Essay takeawayCustomers are heterogeneous, so use the program's data to give differentiated incentives (not one big perk for everyone), and stay mindful of fairness. This connects to the subscription-perks sample question (perks erode margin and attract deal-seekers) and to "loyal customers are less price-sensitive."
Topic ⑫ · Promotion lecture: slides + class recording

Promotion & Advertising: IMC, the 6Ms, Measurement, Media

Plain EnglishPromotion = every way a company talks to customers: ads, social media, store displays, PR, salespeople, word of mouth. The lecture asks: how do you plan a campaign (the 6Ms), does advertising actually work (small short-term effect, big long-term effect), and how do you measure it (attribution vs incrementality vs marketing mix models)?
Where it fits5Cs (analysis) → STP (strategy) → 4Ps (tactics). Promotion is the 4th P. MarComm is not just a TV ad; it's every touchpoint (the Coca-Cola slide: one TV spot vs. dozens of packaging, social, in-store, event touchpoints).

Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC)

The tools around the target consumer

Broadcast (TV/radio) · print · out-of-home (billboards) · sales promotion (price, coupons) · point-of-purchase (store décor, display, packaging) · events & sponsorships · product placement / branded entertainment · personal selling · digital (social, internet, mobile) · PR · word of mouth. Like an orchestra: one coordinated message.

Why IMC, why now

① Consumers are more empowered ② media is fragmented ③ functions (PR, social, retail, CRM) work in silos, wasting spend ④ coordinated spend costs less and works harder. WOM moves real revenue: 13% of sales impact from online WOM, 26% from offline WOM.

The communications plan: the 6Ms

Taco Bell's breakfast problem47% of Americans regularly eat breakfast at a restaurant; quick-service restaurants (QSRs) get 79% of those occasions; breakfast is the only daypart still growing. Taco Bell owned lunch and dinner but had almost no breakfast. Before any ad: which market to target (e.g., McDonald's breakfast "defectors"), then mission, message content and design, media, money, measurement. The mission depends on the target.
Mission: SMART objectivesSpecific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound (Dunkin' example).
✘ "Make our coffee a cultural phenomenon."
~ "Sell more specialty coffee this year."
✔ "Sell 10% more specialty coffee to younger consumers this year."
The creative brief is where strategy becomes creative direction (Colgate, Quaker briefs).
Message must match the momentAwareness (raise attention) → Persuasion (increase interest, change evaluation) → Conversion (drive the action). This is AIDA: Awareness, Interest, Desire, Action. The marketer must know where the customer is in the journey (consider → evaluate → buy → enjoy → advocate → bond, the loyalty loop) to serve the right message.

Does advertising actually work?

Why advertise if the sales effect is small?

① It builds long-term brand equity (associations, positioning, trust). ② Low immediate response ≠ ineffective: it can raise search volume, pricing power, and distribution leverage. ③ Consistency builds mental availability: being the brand that comes to mind at the moment of need (Coca-Cola: consistent messaging for decades; dominant even with no promotion running).

The S-shaped response curve

Implications: spending too little is wasted (below the threshold); there is a sweet spot; past it, diminishing returns. Money = "where are you on the curve?"

Question it answersHowWatch out
Attribution (multi-touch)How to split credit for a sale across touchpoints (display ad → search ad → click → buy)User-level data on impressions, clicks, conversions (first click = Instagram ad, last click = search ad; an influencer's post)Mostly online only; credit ≠ cause

Media strategy: four types

Paid = you buy it (TV, print, radio, display, direct mail, paid search, retail/channel)
Owned = you control it (website, microsite, blog, brand community, social pages, app)
Earned = you win it (WOM, comments, mentions, blogs, forums, reviews)
Sold / Retail media = you sell it (invite other marketers onto your owned media)
Retail media networks = retailers sell ad space on their site/app (even to competitors) using first-party purchase data, shown at or near the moment of purchase. $71B US in 2026 (+18%); ~74% controlled by Amazon, Walmart, Target; the #1 fastest-growing digital ad channel. Others: Costco, eBay, Instacart, Macy's, Best Buy, Home Depot, Kroger.

Does ad spend create word of mouth?

Six-year study (Lovett, Peres & Xu)A 10% increase in TV ad spend → ~69,000 more brand mentions; a 1% increase in WOM → only 0.01–0.06% more sales. On average the lift is small, but it's much bigger in some categories (entertainment, sports): "your mileage may vary."
Super Bowl & social$8M per 30-second Super Bowl LX spot (some >$10M); Budweiser has run 142 Super Bowl ads, the most of any brand; WOM spikes the week of the game, then decays fast. Best social ROI per marketers: TikTok 42%, Instagram 33%, YouTube 19%, Snap 6%. YouTube = best value: videos keep earning views for 12–24 months.
Gen AI in advertising87% of marketers use gen AI in at least one workflow (51% in 2024); 83% of ad executives use AI in creative (60%). But only 45% of Gen Z/Millennials feel positive about AI-made ads, while 82% of executives assume they do: a perception gap. Measure consumer response; don't assume.

From the class recording

Marketing's "existential crisis": performance vs brandTypical advertising elasticity ≈ 0.1 vs price ≈ 2.6; sales force, promotions, distribution, and innovation all move short-term sales more. But don't over-index on them: price promotions erode margins and train price sensitivity, and pure performance marketing (measurable, short-term conversion) starves brand marketing (harder to measure, long-term). Brand equity drives purchase, and advertising is still the main way to build it.

IMC, customer at the center

Paid (TV, digital, social influencers) · owned (website, brochures) · earned (WOM). Earned = the gold standard, the strongest driver of purchase, but it needs paid media first to create awareness. New frontiers: retail media networks (point-of-purchase targeting with rich data) and AI search (ChatGPT), growing fast and not yet in the frameworks.

Taco Bell: three possible targets

① people already eating breakfast out elsewhere (convert from competitors) ② existing Taco Bell lunch/dinner customers (extend the occasion) ③ people who don't eat breakfast out (create a new behavior). Each needs a different campaign. Actual: the "Routine Republic" ad targeted habitual breakfast eaters; measured with Waze (did exposed drivers visit a store?): 15% visited, 1 in 7 came back. Real movement, but breakfast habits are hard to shift.
Hierarchy of effects & the funnelAwareness → persuasion → conversion → loyalty. The funnel is an imperfect metaphor but useful for diagnosing where to focus: campaign type, message, and budget differ by goal (awareness vs conversion vs retention) and audience (new vs existing customers). Without a specific, measurable mission, you can't tell whether a campaign worked.
Measurement pointWhat was said in class
Last-touch problemCrediting only the final click ignores all the earlier brand building. Evolution: last-touch → multi-touch attribution → renewed interest in MMM.
MMMEconometric: regress aggregate sales on all marketing inputs at once to isolate each channel. Must handle multicollinearity (channels move together) and endogeneity (spend responds to sales).
IncrementalityThe marginal value of an exposure beyond everything else running. Method: randomized controlled experiments (test vs control), mostly feasible online.
PrivacyGDPR (Europe), CCPA (California) and possible US rules push firms away from individual-level data, toward MMM. Watermarking (LG Ads, a $2B ad business): embed identifiers at each touchpoint below the privacy layer; limited by publisher cooperation.
S-curve & long-term effectsLittle effect below a frequency threshold (~3 exposures), then accelerating returns, then diminishing returns. Why Coca-Cola still advertises despite near-universal awareness: if it stops while competitors continue, salience erodes. Long-term effects missed by short-term metrics: brand equity, channel pricing power, even HR (recruiting) and analyst perception.
Super Bowl lessonsBudweiser: sales spikes came not just at the Super Bowl but at later sporting events (March Madness, regular season). The ad built top-of-mind awareness that activated at relevant moments, invisible to short-window measurement. Duolingo = IMC done right: tested organic social ads to find a winner → bought a 5-second Super Bowl slot → pushed in-app reminders to existing users at the same time. Paid + owned + earned, coordinated for acquisition and retention.
