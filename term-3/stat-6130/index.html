HOW TO USE THIS LESSON

Start with the story, then follow the JMP clicks

The lender collects a percentage of each merchant’s credit-card sales. After six months, the project asks whether repayment is on schedule and whether simple borrower information can help predict performance.

PRSM = 2 × (repaid after 6 months ÷ total due)
1 ≈ on schedule　 ·　<1 behind　 ·　>1 ahead

Use the Team 04 CSV in JMP. For the regression questions, use the 627 valid observed cases after excluding one unmistakable recording anomaly. Keep the anomaly visible and document why it is excluded.

QUESTION 1 · LEAST-SQUARES REGRESSION

Is repayment close to the six-month target?

The ideal plan repays half of the total amount due by month six.

Total due: $20,000loan amount including fees
→
Half due by month 6$10,000 repaid
→
Ideal liney = 0.5x
Expected: ŷ = 0 + 0.50x
intercept = $0　 ·　slope = $0.50 repaid for each $1 due

JMP: find the unusual record before fitting

Open the Team 04 data
In JMP choose File → Open and open the CSV. Confirm the repayment and total-due columns are numeric. The response column is Amt.Repaid.at.6.Months; the explanatory column is Total.Amt.to.be.Repaid.
Create a repayment-to-due check column
Choose Cols → New Column, name it RepaidToDueRatio, set Modeling Type to Continuous, then click Formula. Divide Amt.Repaid.at.6.Months by Total.Amt.to.be.Repaid. Sort this new column from largest to smallest to inspect extreme records.
Exclude the single implausible record from the fit
Row 86 records $2,513,660 repaid against $96,720 due, about 26 times the obligation. This is a likely data-entry error. Select that row and choose Rows → Exclude/Unexclude so it stays in the table but does not affect the analysis. Do not exclude records only because they are inconvenient.
Why this matters: one extreme y-value can pull the regression line toward itself and distort the slope, intercept, and residuals. After exclusion, 627 observed loans remain for the analysis.

JMP: fit the line

Analyze → Fit Y by X → Y: Amt.Repaid.at.6.Months → X: Total.Amt.to.be.Repaid → OK
Show the least-squares line
Both columns are Continuous, so JMP opens the Bivariate platform. Click the red triangle beside Bivariate Fit and choose Fit Line. Read the equation, R², and RMSE in the Linear Fit report.
Ask JMP for the diagnostic plots
Click the red triangle beside Linear Fit and choose Plot Residuals. You’ll see residual-by-predicted, actual-by-predicted, residual-by-row, residual-by-X, and a normal quantile plot. The course asks you to use these plots to judge the assumptions.
Fitted slope0.4864
Fitted intercept−$2,271.88
R²91.45%
RMSE$8,331
Six-month repayment versus total due
Dots show the 627 valid observed cases. Gold dashed line is the ideal half-repaid target. Blue line is the fitted regression.

The fitted equation is ŷ = −2,271.88 + 0.486412x. A $100,000 increase in total due is associated with about $48,641 more repaid by month six. That is slightly below the $50,000 expected under a perfectly even 12-month repayment schedule. The negative intercept is where the best-fit line crosses the y-axis; it does not mean a merchant literally pays a negative amount.

Residual diagnosis: the residuals fan out more for larger loans, and the tails depart from a straight line on the normal quantile plot. Constant spread and normality are questionable. The line is a useful average summary, but its prediction errors are not equally sized across loan amounts.
What to tell your group: “The ideal six-month relationship is half of the total due, so slope 0.5 and intercept 0. Our cleaned regression slope is 0.486, a little below target. R² is high, but the residual plots show increasing spread and heavy tails, so we should be careful with individual-loan predictions.”
If a loan has $40,000 total due, the ideal six-month repayment is $20,000. That’s exactly what a slope of 0.5 means.
QUESTION 2 · BUILD AND REGRESS PRSM

Put every loan on the same performance scale

PRSM compares what was repaid with what “on schedule” would mean at six months.

PRSM = 2 × (repaid ÷ total due)
on schedule → 1　 ·　behind → below 1　 ·　ahead → above 1

JMP: create PRSM

Add a new PRSM column
Choose Cols → New Column. Name it PRSM, set Modeling Type to Continuous, choose Formula, then enter 2 * Amt.Repaid.at.6.Months / Total.Amt.to.be.Repaid. Rows with missing repayments should remain missing.
Fit PRSM by total amount due
Choose Analyze → Fit Y by X. Put PRSM in Y and Total.Amt.to.be.Repaid in X. Click OK, then the Bivariate Fit red triangle → Fit Line. Open the Linear Fit red triangle → Plot Residuals.

If loan sizes perform equally, the average PRSM should be about 1 at every size. That ideal relationship is a flat line: intercept 1, slope 0.

Fitted intercept0.7950
Fitted slope0.000000677
R²4.10%
RMSE0.1833
PRSM versus total due
Horizontal gold dashed line is PRSM = 1, the on-schedule reference. The fitted line rises slightly.

For each additional $10,000 due, predicted PRSM rises by about 0.00677. The slope is positive (95% interval about 0.000000420 to 0.000000934; p < 0.0001), so the data point in the opposite direction from the lender’s concern that larger loans underperform. Still, size explains only about 4.1% of the variation. Statistical evidence of a small trend does not make loan size a strong individual predictor.

Residual diagnosis: the shared team analysis shows the residual quantiles tracking the reference line reasonably well, with no obvious row-order pattern. Independence is still an assumption about different merchants. The model is a useful rough summary, but the low R² and RMSE of 0.1833 show wide variation around it.
What to tell your group: “PRSM rescales six-month repayment so 1 means on schedule. The estimated slope is small and positive, not negative. Larger loans do not show lower average PRSM in this analysis, but loan size explains little of the differences between individual merchants.”
PRSM = 0.70 means the merchant has repaid about 70% of the amount expected by month six, so the loan is behind schedule.
QUESTION 3 · DIVERSIFICATION AND NORMAL PROBABILITY

One large loan or sixteen smaller loans?

The lender needs at least $60,000 back by month six, out of $160,000 total due.

Required average PRSM = 2 × ($60,000 ÷ $160,000) = 0.75

Use the Question 2 regression to estimate the average PRSM for each loan size, and use its RMSE (0.1833) as the approximate standard deviation of individual outcomes.

The total due stays at $160,000. Each merchant’s loan is $160,000 divided by the number of merchants.
Merchants16
Mean PRSM per loan0.8018
SD of average PRSM0.0458
Chance of ≥ $60,00087.07%
Distribution of average PRSM for 16 loans
The shaded right tail represents the chance the average PRSM is at least 0.75. Move the slider to compare diversification.
Assumptions: individual PRSM values are approximately normal, merchant outcomes are independent, and the regression’s RMSE applies to each loan. If all merchants are hit by the same economic shock, the portfolio benefit will be smaller.
Recommendation: spread the lending across 16 merchants. The estimated chance of receiving at least $60,000 is about 87.1%, compared with 79.8% for one $160,000 loan, an increase of about 7.2 percentage points.
Averaging 16 independent outcomes shrinks the standard deviation by √16 = 4. The mean stays near the target, but the average is less likely to swing far below it.

A 100-point increase in FICO is associated with about 0.1066 higher average PRSM. The intercept predicts PRSM at FICO = 0, which is outside the useful range, so it has little practical meaning. FICO is the best single predictor among these choices, but it still explains only about 15% of the variation.

JMP: save predictions for the 1,050 incomplete cases

Fit PRSM by FICO on observed cases
Use Analyze → Fit Y by X, Y = PRSM, X = FICO, then Fit Line. Compare candidate predictors using the same observed rows. FICO has the highest R² and lowest RMSE here.
Save the predicted values
From the red triangle beside Linear Fit, choose Save Predicteds. JMP adds a predicted PRSM column. Filter to rows where Amt.Repaid.at.6.Months is missing, then keep only CaseNumber and the predicted PRSM values.
Prepare the Excel submission
The project requires exactly two columns named CaseNumber and Prediction, with only the 1,050 incomplete cases. A two-column workbook is supplied separately. Check the row count before submitting it in Canvas.
Team-file check: the shared write-up has a small slope typo in one sentence. The fitted slope is 0.4864118 (the equation in the second write-up has the correct rounded value).
What to tell your group: “We compared seven one-variable regressions on the same clean cases. FICO has the best R² and RMSE, so we use it. The fitted model is PRSM = 0.21334 + 0.0010662 × FICO. It gives a useful average prediction, but individual predictions still have substantial error.”
For FICO = 600, predicted PRSM is about 0.853. This is a model-based average prediction, not a guarantee about one merchant.
GROUP DISCUSSION RECAP

Four answers in one minute

1 · Expected repayment line
At six months, the target is half the total due: slope 0.5, intercept 0.
2 · Performance ratio
PRSM = 2 × repaid / total due. On-schedule is 1. The size slope is small, positive, and statistically detectable, but R² is only 4.1%.
3 · Loan portfolio
The model estimates about 79.8% chance for one large loan and 87.1% for sixteen independent small loans to clear the $60,000 six-month target.
4 · Missing-value prediction
FICO has the highest R² (15.3%) and lowest RMSE (0.1722) among the seven candidate predictors. Save only the 1,050 missing-case predictions.
